The solar wind is a continuous stream of charged particles, primarily electrons and protons, released from the upper atmosphere of the Sun, known as the corona. This plasma stream travels outward into the solar system at velocities varying between 300 and 800 kilometers per second. When the solar wind encounters a planet with an intrinsic magnetic field, such as Earth, the magnetic field deflects the charged particles, carving out a protective cavity in space known as the magnetosphere. The boundary where the solar wind's supersonic speed drops abruptly to subsonic speed before deflecting around the magnetosphere is known as the bow shock. While the magnetosphere shields the planet's surface from harmful ionizing radiation, solar wind plasma can still enter through dynamic regions such as the polar cusps. As energetic particles travel down magnetic field lines into the upper atmosphere, they collide with atoms of oxygen and nitrogen, exciting these gases to higher energy states. When the excited atmospheric atoms return to their ground states, they emit photons of light, producing auroral displays such as the Aurora Borealis and Aurora Australis. Geomagnetic storms occur when significant disturbances in the solar wind, often triggered by coronal mass ejections or high-speed solar wind streams originating from coronal holes, transfer immense energy into Earth's space environment. These storms can induce hazardous electric currents in ground-based power distribution grids, disrupt radio communication networks, and degrade satellite navigation systems by causing intense ionospheric irregularities.
